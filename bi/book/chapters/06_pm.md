# Part 6: Project and Product

## The role in one page

Two jobs share this part because they share a vocabulary. A **project manager** is accountable for delivering an agreed scope on time and within budget: plans, schedules, costs, risks, and keeping people informed. A **product manager** is accountable for deciding what is worth building and for proving afterwards that it helped: problems, priorities, measures of success. In a small company one person does both; in a large one they are neighbours who argue usefully.

**Who you work with.** Engineers and designers who do the work, a sponsor or executive who pays for it, customers or users who have the problem, finance who ask about budget, and support or sales who bring the complaints. Most of your work is conversation; the numbers are there so those conversations are about evidence instead of volume.

**A typical day.** Check the board: what moved, what is stuck, what has been "in progress" for nine days. Talk to the person whose item is stuck and ask what is in the way, not why they are slow. Re-rank two backlog items because a customer call changed an assumption. Update a risk's probability. Write three sentences for the sponsor: where we are, what changed, what I need from you. Spend some time on a document that states a problem before anyone proposes a solution.

**What good looks like.**

- Everyone can say what the next most important thing is, and why.
- Work is cut small enough to finish inside a sprint, and finishes.
- Forecasts are ranges ("most likely about 4 sprints, between 3 and 6"), never a single date stated as fact.
- Surprises are rare because risks were written down and priced.
- A shipped feature is checked against the measure it promised to move.

**What it is NOT.** It is not taking notes and chasing people. It is not drawing a Gantt chart and defending it. It is not using a score to make a decision for you: prioritisation numbers rank, and a person decides. And it is not a title that grants authority; you will often lead without it.

**Honest hard parts.**

- Estimates are guesses, and the metrics built from them look more exact than they are.
- People game any number that is used to judge them, so how you use a metric matters as much as how you compute it.
- You are often blamed for decisions you advised against, and credited for results you did not cause.
- Saying "not now" to good ideas, politely and repeatedly, is most of the job.

## How to use this part

Four levels, at about 5 hours a week:

| Level | Focus | Reading and practice | Calendar time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Foundations | Problems, scope, the board, RICE, WSJF, velocity | about 10 hours | 2 weeks |
| 2 Practitioner | Flow metrics, forecasting, critical path, earned value | about 15 hours | 3 weeks |
| 3 Advanced | Risk exposure, OKRs, funnels, retention, North Star, experiments, effort tracking | about 15 hours | 3 weeks |
| 4 Expert | Judgement, gaming, honest reporting, running a portfolio | about 10 hours | 2 weeks |

Add about 25 hours for the three capstones.

**What you need.** A spreadsheet, a notebook or index cards, and a real or invented project of your own: a home renovation, a club event, a small app, a study plan. Every technique here works on a wall of sticky notes. A board tool (free tiers exist) is convenient but not required. Do every calculation by hand once before you let a tool do it, so you know what the tool's number means, and so you can tell when it is wrong.

**Use one running project.** Invent or choose a project with about 12 work items, 3 sprints or phases, 4 risks and 3 goals. Reuse it at every level: rank it at Level 1, measure its flow at Level 2, price its risks at Level 3, defend its report at Level 4. A single project you know well teaches more than ten toy examples.

## Level 1: Foundations (beginner)

### What you will be able to do

- You can write a one-paragraph problem statement before proposing any solution.
- You can place a work item on a board and say what each status means.
- You can compute a RICE score and a WSJF score by hand.
- You can sort a release into Must, Should, Could and Won't, and check the Must share.
- You can read velocity and a burndown chart and say whether a sprint is on plan.
- You can explain why scores rank work but do not make decisions.

### Core ideas

#### Problem first, solution second

A one-page product requirements document (PRD) has five parts: who has the problem, what the pain is, how you will know it is solved, what you will build, and what you will **not** build. Write the problem and the measure before the solution. A weak version: "Add an export button." A strong one: "Finance staff re-key the weekly operations table into a spreadsheet, about 40 minutes a week each, with typing errors. Success: 80% of the 10 finance users export instead of re-keying within a month. Not in scope: scheduled exports, PDF." The strong version can be tested and can be argued with. The "not in scope" line is your defence against scope creep, the slow addition of unplanned work.

#### The board and its statuses

A board is columns of cards. A common set of statuses is: backlog (not committed), todo (committed to the current sprint), doing, review and done. Items have a kind: epic (large, split later), story (user-visible value), task, bug, or milestone (a point in time with no duration of its own). Record the date a card moves to doing and the date it moves to done; those dates feed every flow metric in Level 2, so keep the board honest: if work starts, move the card. An item that cannot finish inside one sprint is too big; split it until it can. A card should have an owner and a title that says an outcome ("Export weekly table to CSV"), not an activity ("Work on export").

#### RICE: ranking features by expected value

**RICE = Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort.** Reach is people affected per period. Impact uses a scale: 0.25 minimal, 0.5 low, 1 medium, 2 high, 3 massive. Confidence is 0 to 1 (0.8 = 80% sure). Effort is person-months or points. Example, feature A: reach 100, impact 2, confidence 0.8, effort 4: 100 x 2 x 0.8 / 4 = **40**. Feature B: reach 50, impact 3, confidence 1, effort 1: 50 x 3 x 1 / 1 = **150**. B ranks first despite reaching half as many people, because it is cheap and certain. Confidence is the honesty term: a wild guess of 0.3 pulls an exciting idea down until you have evidence.

#### WSJF: ranking by cost of delay

**WSJF = (User value + Time criticality + Risk reduction) / Job size.** Score the three numerator terms on a relative scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20) and use story points as job size. Take A with value 8, time criticality 5, risk reduction 3 and 2 points: (8 + 5 + 3) / 2 = **8**. Take B with 2, 2, 2 and 4 points: 6 / 4 = **1.5**. WSJF puts A first. For the same pair, a RICE ranking could differ; that is not a bug, since the methods ask different questions. RICE asks what gives the most expected benefit per effort; WSJF asks what loses the most if we wait. Use RICE for choosing features, WSJF when timing matters (a deadline, a security fix).

#### MoSCoW scope and velocity in brief

**MoSCoW** sorts scope: Must, Should, Could, Won't (this time). A healthy release keeps Must items below about 60% of the effort so there is room for surprises. **Velocity** is story points *completed* per sprint. If a sprint committed 10 points and finished 8, velocity is 8; the 2 carried over do not count. A **burndown** plots points remaining each day against a straight ideal line from the committed total to zero. For 10 points over a 10-day sprint, the ideal is 5 remaining on day 5. If your line sits above the ideal you are behind; a flat line followed by a cliff means work was left until the end or the board was not updated.

### Practice on your own

1. Write a one-page PRD for something you actually want, using the five parts. Show it to one person and ask them what is unclear.
2. Make a board on paper or in any tool. Put 12 items for your running project into it, each with a kind, an owner and an outcome-style title. Split any item you could not finish in a sprint.
3. Score six features with RICE in a spreadsheet. Change one confidence by 0.1 and see whether the order changes.
4. Score the same six with WSJF. Note where the two rankings differ and write why.
5. Tag every item Must, Should, Could or Won't and compute the Must share by points.
6. Track one real sprint of a week or two: write points committed, completed and remaining each day, then draw the burndown.

### Exercises

1. Rewrite "Improve reporting" as a problem statement with a measure and a non-goal.
2. Compute RICE for reach 200, impact 1, confidence 0.5, effort 5.
3. Compute WSJF for value 13, time criticality 8, risk reduction 2, size 5.
4. Two items score RICE 40 and 38. Should the first always go first? Explain.
5. A sprint commits 12 points and finishes 9, with 3 carried over. What is its velocity? What goes wrong if you count carried points?
6. A release plan has 30 points of Must, 15 Should, 5 Could. What is the Must share, and is it healthy?
7. The sponsor demands that a Won't item go in. Describe two honest responses.

### Answers and hints

2. 200 x 1 x 0.5 / 5 = **20**.
3. (13 + 8 + 2) / 5 = 23 / 5 = **4.6**.
4. No. The difference is within the uncertainty of the inputs; change one confidence by 0.1 and the order can flip. Decide on other grounds (dependencies, risk, learning).
5. Velocity is 9. Counting carried points would report 12, so your forecasts would be too optimistic every sprint.
6. 30 / 50 = **60%**. That is at the limit; it leaves little room for surprises, so consider moving one Must down or cutting scope.
7. Hint: make the trade visible ("to add this, which Must moves out, or do we move the date?"), or ask what measure it would move.

### Checkpoint

Answer aloud:

1. What are the five parts of a one-page PRD?
2. What does each letter of RICE stand for?
3. When would you prefer WSJF to RICE?
4. What is a burndown's ideal line?
5. Why does velocity count only completed points?
6. What does the Must share tell you?
7. Why do board dates matter?

Common mistakes:

- Starting with a solution and finding a problem to fit.
- Treating a RICE score as the decision.
- Counting partially finished work as velocity.
- Comparing the velocity of two teams (points are a private scale).
- Letting cards sit in doing for weeks without moving them.

## Level 2: Practitioner (intermediate)

### What you will be able to do

- You can compute cycle time, lead time and throughput, and quote a percentile you could promise.
- You can use Little's law to judge whether you have too much work in progress.
- You can forecast completion as a range from velocity.
- You can find the critical path and the float of a small network by hand.
- You can compute PV, EV, AC, CPI, SPI, EAC and TCPI and say in one sentence what they mean.
- You can say what each of these tools cannot know.

### Core ideas

#### Cycle time, lead time and percentiles

**Cycle time** runs from started to done. **Lead time** runs from created to done, so it includes waiting in the backlog. Suppose ten finished items had cycle times of 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 14 days. The average is 59 / 10 = 5.9 days, the median is 5, and the 85th percentile is about 8.7 days (interpolating between the 8th value, 8, and the 9th, 9; tools differ slightly in how they interpolate). Quote the percentile: "85% of items finish within about 9 days" is a promise you can plan with, whereas "average 5.9" hides the 14-day outlier that will spoil someone's week. **Throughput** is items finished per week; look at the last eight weeks, not one. A short cycle time with a long lead time means work waits, not that people are slow.

#### Work in progress and Little's law

**Little's law: average WIP = throughput x average cycle time.** Say your team finished 8 items in the last 28 days (throughput 8 / 28 = 0.29 per day) and the average cycle time is 5.9 days. Expected WIP is 0.29 x 5.9, about 1.7 items. If the board shows 5 items in doing or review, you are carrying about three times the work the flow explains: things are stuck, or you start more than you finish. The remedy is counter-intuitive: **limit WIP**. Finish before starting. Cycle time falls because work stops queueing behind other work. A **cumulative flow diagram** (items in each status, stacked over time) shows the same thing visually: bands that widen over time mark the state where work is piling up.

#### Forecasting as a range

**Forecast = remaining points / velocity.** Suppose the last three finished sprints completed 8, 10 and 12 points (average 10) and 45 points remain. Likely: 45 / 10 = 4.5 sprints. Fast case (best sprint, 12): 3.75, about 3.8. Slow case (worst, 8): 5.6. With two-week sprints the likely finish is about 63 days away, and the honest statement is "between 53 and 79 days, most likely about 9 weeks". Present the range, name what would shift it (scope added, someone away), and update it each sprint. Ranges are not a sign of weakness; a single date is false precision. Count only story, task and bug points as remaining, because epics are containers and milestones have no work.

#### The critical path

Give items a duration in days and dependencies. For each, compute earliest start and finish (ES, EF), then latest start and finish (LS, LF) that do not delay the project. **Float = LS - ES.** Items with zero float form the **critical path**. Worked network: A (3 days), B (2, after A), C (6, after A), D (4, after B and C). ES: A 0, B 3, C 3, D = max(5, 9) = 9. Total duration 13. Path A, C, D = 3 + 6 + 4 = 13 is critical; B has 4 days of float (it could start as late as day 7). Delay A, C or D and the project slips; delay B by up to four days and nothing changes. A loop (A needs B, B needs A) has no schedule at all; a good tool refuses it. And a schedule computed from planned durations shows the plan's shape, not today's reality, until you update it with progress.

#### Earned value

Take a budget at completion (BAC) of 1,000. Item A has planned cost 400 and is done, having actually cost 500. Item B has planned cost 600, is exactly halfway through its planned duration, and has cost 250 so far. **PV** (planned value) = 400 + 600 x 0.5 = 700. **EV** (earned value, the planned cost of finished work, with the 0/100 rule that unfinished items earn nothing) = 400. **AC** (actual cost) = 750. **CPI** = EV / AC = 0.533: each dollar spent earned 53 cents of planned work. **SPI** = EV / PV = 0.571: behind schedule. **EAC** = BAC / CPI = 1,875 if the trend continues; ETC = EAC - AC = 1,125; VAC = BAC - EAC = -875. **TCPI** = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC) = 600 / 250 = 2.4: to finish on budget you would need 2.4 times the efficiency you have shown. Far above 1 means the budget is probably gone; say so early. Earned value is only as good as the actual costs you record: if nobody logs the spend, CPI means nothing.

### Practice on your own

1. For your running project, list finished items with start and finish dates. Compute cycle time for each, then the average, median and 85th percentile in a spreadsheet.
2. Count how many items are in doing today. Compute expected WIP from Little's law and compare. If actual is double expected, name two items to finish before starting anything new.
3. Compute the last three sprints' velocity (or invent three plausible values) and forecast the remaining work as a fast, likely and slow case in weeks.
4. Draw a dependency network of eight items on paper. Compute ES, EF, LS, LF and float by hand, circle the critical path, then check it in a spreadsheet.
5. Give each item a planned cost and a status. Compute PV, EV, AC, CPI, SPI, EAC and TCPI for a date you choose. Write one sentence for a sponsor.
6. Draw a cumulative flow diagram from one week of daily counts per status. Look for the band that widens.

### Exercises

1. Cycle times in days: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9. Compute the average and median. Which better describes a typical item, and why?
2. Throughput is 6 items per 20 days and average cycle time is 4 days. What WIP does Little's law expect? The board shows 6 in progress. Interpret.
3. Remaining 36 points; recent velocities 9, 12, 6. Give the fast, likely and slow number of sprints.
4. Network: A (2) first; B (4) and C (3) after A; D (2) after B; E (3) after C and D. Find the total duration, the critical path and each item's float.
5. BAC 5,000; EV 1,500; AC 2,500; PV 2,000. Compute CPI, SPI, EAC and TCPI.
6. Explain in two sentences to a non-specialist what the CPI in exercise 5 says.
7. Your CPI is 1.2 but SPI is 0.7. Describe what that combination might mean and what you would ask.
8. A teammate says "the critical path is the important tasks". Correct them.

### Answers and hints

1. Sum = 27 over 7 items, average 3.86; median (the 4th of seven) is 3. The median describes a typical item better here because the 9 pulls the average up. Quote a high percentile when promising.
2. Throughput 0.3 per day x 4 days = 1.2 items. Six in progress is five times the flow explains: work is stuck or you are starting too much.
3. Average 9. Likely 36 / 9 = 4; fast 36 / 12 = 3; slow 36 / 6 = 6 sprints.
4. A 0 to 2. B 2 to 6, C 2 to 5. D 6 to 8. E starts at max(5, 8) = 8, ends 11. Total 11. Critical path A, B, D, E (2 + 4 + 2 + 3 = 11). C: finishes at 5 but E cannot start until 8, so 3 days of float.
5. CPI = 1,500 / 2,500 = 0.6; SPI = 1,500 / 2,000 = 0.75; EAC = 5,000 / 0.6 = 8,333; TCPI = 3,500 / 2,500 = 1.4.
6. Hint: "We have done 60 cents of planned work for each dollar spent; if that continues the project will cost about 8,300 instead of 5,000."
7. Under budget but behind. Maybe the team is small and careful, or blocked and not spending. Ask what is waiting and on whom.

### Checkpoint

Answer aloud:

1. Difference between cycle time and lead time?
2. Why quote a percentile?
3. State Little's law.
4. Why give a range, not a date?
5. What is float, and what does zero float mean?
6. What does the 0/100 rule do to earned value?
7. What does a TCPI of 1.8 tell you?

Common mistakes:

- Reporting the average and hiding the tail.
- Starting new work to look busy while old work waits.
- Treating a forecast date as a commitment.
- Reading the critical path as "the important tasks".
- Not updating actual cost, so CPI means nothing.

## Level 3: Advanced

### What you will be able to do

- You can keep a risk register and compute exposure and expected cost.
- You can write objectives and key results and score progress honestly.
- You can compute funnel conversion and cohort retention and read the curves.
- You can choose a North Star metric, break it into inputs and add a guardrail.
- You can say whether an A/B difference is likely real or noise.
- You can track effort and automate routine reporting without hiding judgement.

### Core ideas

#### Risk exposure and the register

A **risk** is something that might happen and would matter; an **issue** is something that already has. A register lists each risk with an owner, a probability (P), an impact (I), a response and a trigger. Score P and I from 1 to 5 and take **exposure = P x I**. A key supplier delay with P = 4 and I = 5 scores 20; a minor tool outage with P = 2 and I = 2 scores 4. Sort by exposure and spend your attention at the top.

Where you can put money on impact, use **expected monetary value: EMV = probability x cost**. A 30% chance of a 20,000 rework is worth 6,000. Four risks with EMVs of 6,000, 2,500, 1,000 and 500 sum to 10,000, which is a reasonable contingency reserve to ask for, and a sentence a sponsor can act on. Responses come in four kinds: **avoid** (change the plan), **mitigate** (reduce P or I), **transfer** (insurance, contract) and **accept** (write down that you chose to). Distinguish mitigation, which you do now, from contingency, which you do only if the risk fires. Review the register every week; a risk nobody has touched for a month is probably stale or hidden.

#### OKRs: objectives and key results

An **objective** is a qualitative direction ("Make the first week painless for new customers"). **Key results** are two to five measurable outcomes that show it happened ("Raise week-one activation from 30% to 50%"). Progress on a key result is **(current - start) / (target - start)**. Take a key result "weekly active users from 200 to 500", currently 320: (320 - 200) / (500 - 200) = 0.4, or 40%. With three key results at 40%, 75% and 20% the objective is about (0.4 + 0.75 + 0.2) / 3 = 45%.

Write key results as outcomes, not activities: "launch the new onboarding" is a task; "activation reaches 50%" is a result. Aim for ambitious targets, where roughly 70% achievement is a good quarter, and do not link scores to pay, or people will set safe targets. Review quarterly, and in the review ask what you learned, not only what the score was.

#### Funnels and cohort retention

A **funnel** counts people through steps. 2,000 visits, 400 sign-ups, 120 activated (did the first useful thing), 30 paid. Step conversions: 400 / 2,000 = 20%, 120 / 400 = 30%, 30 / 120 = 25%. Overall: 30 / 2,000 = 1.5%. Fix the step with the biggest drop that you can actually influence, and check the definition of each step: "activated" must mean a real first value, not a login.

**Retention** asks whether people come back. Group users by the week they joined (a **cohort**) and count who is active in later weeks. Cohort of 200: 90 active in week 1 (45%), 50 in week 4 (25%), 44 in week 8 (22%). The curve flattens near 22%, which suggests a core of users who keep getting value. A curve that falls to near zero means people try the product and leave, and no amount of acquisition fixes that. Compare cohorts of different dates, not only the overall number: a growing user base can hide worsening retention.

#### North Star metric and guardrails

A **North Star metric** is the single measure that best captures the value customers get, and that predicts long-term business health. Revenue is rarely it; it lags. Good ones count delivered value: for a task tool, "tasks completed per week by active accounts". Break it into inputs your teams can move: 1,200 tasks per week = 150 active accounts x 8 tasks each. Now one team owns breadth (more accounts become active) and another depth (more tasks per account), and each can see how it contributes.

Add **guardrail metrics** that must not get worse while you chase the North Star: support tickets per 100 accounts, error rate, unsubscribe rate. A team that doubles tasks per account by adding nagging emails has improved the number and harmed the product; the guardrail shows it. Write down what the metric does not measure.

#### Is the difference real? Experiments in brief

You change a sign-up page. Version A converts 40 of 1,000 visitors (4.0%), version B 52 of 1,000 (5.2%). Is B better? The rough standard error of each rate is sqrt(p(1-p)/n): for A, sqrt(0.04 x 0.96 / 1,000) = 0.0062; for B, sqrt(0.052 x 0.948 / 1,000) = 0.0070. The standard error of the difference is sqrt(0.0062^2 + 0.0070^2) = 0.0094. The observed difference is 0.012, which is 0.012 / 0.0094 = 1.3 standard errors, well short of the roughly 2 you want for a rough 95% level. Treat it as inconclusive: keep running, or accept that you cannot detect an effect this small with this traffic. Decide the sample size and the metric before you start, run for whole weeks, and do not stop the moment the numbers look good. Randomise who sees which version.

#### Tracking effort, and automating the routine

Time tracking has one honest use at this level: connecting actual effort to cost. If an item's recorded hours are 10 at a rate of 80, its actual cost is 800, which replaces a guess in your earned-value calculation. Track effort to learn how estimates compare with reality, never to monitor individuals; people who feel watched stop recording honestly.

Automation suits repetitive reporting: a weekly digest of what moved, a nudge when a card has been in doing for more than twice the 85th percentile cycle time, a reminder when a risk review is overdue. Rules should notify and propose, not decide: "this card looks stuck" is useful; "this card was automatically cancelled" is a surprise. Keep each rule written down with its owner, and remove rules nobody reads.

### Practice on your own

1. Write a register of six risks for your running project with P, I, exposure, owner, response and trigger. Add EMV for two of them.
2. Write one objective and three key results for your project. Compute progress for a made-up current value of each.
3. Draw a funnel of four steps for something you run (a newsletter, a club). Compute step and overall conversion.
4. Build a cohort table from invented or real data (three cohorts, four weeks). Plot the retention curves.
5. Choose a North Star for your project, break it into two inputs, and name one guardrail.
6. Take two conversion rates from any source and compute the rough z value as in the text. Decide what you would do.
7. Log your effort on real work for one week. Compare your estimate with the actual for three items.

### Exercises

1. A risk has P = 3 and I = 4 on 1 to 5 scales. What is its exposure? Another has P = 5, I = 1. Which do you handle first, and is exposure alone enough to decide?
2. A 25% chance of a 40,000 delay and a 10% chance of a 60,000 penalty. Compute each EMV and the total reserve.
3. Key result: reduce defects per release from 20 to 8; current 14. Compute progress.
4. 5,000 visits, 15% sign up, 40% of sign-ups activate, 10% of the activated pay. How many pay? Overall conversion?
5. Cohort of 300: weeks 1, 4, 8 active are 150, 90, 60. Give retention percentages and describe the curve.
6. A: 90 conversions of 2,000. B: 120 of 2,000. Compute rates, the standard error of the difference and the z value.
7. Propose a North Star and guardrail for a library lending app.
8. A manager wants to use tracked hours to rank team members. Reply.

### Answers and hints

1. 3 x 4 = 12. The other is 5. Handle 12 first, but exposure ignores things such as deadline (a risk that fires next week) and whether a cheap fix exists; add trigger dates.
2. 0.25 x 40,000 = 10,000; 0.10 x 60,000 = 6,000; total 16,000.
3. (14 - 20) / (8 - 20) = (-6) / (-12) = 0.5, or 50%.
4. 5,000 x 0.15 = 750; x 0.40 = 300; x 0.10 = 30 pay. Overall 30 / 5,000 = 0.6%.
5. 50%, 30%, 20%. It is still falling at week 8, so check weeks 12 and 16 before concluding there is a stable core.
6. A = 4.5%, B = 6.0%. SE(A) = sqrt(0.045 x 0.955 / 2,000) = 0.00464; SE(B) = sqrt(0.06 x 0.94 / 2,000) = 0.00531; SE difference = sqrt(0.00464^2 + 0.00531^2) = 0.00705; difference 0.015, so z = 2.1. Likely real, if the test was planned and randomised.
8. Hint: tracked hours measure recorded time, not value; ranking people will make everyone log what looks good. Offer to use them for estimates instead.

### Checkpoint

Answer aloud:

1. Risk versus issue?
2. How do you compute exposure and EMV?
3. What are the four risk responses?
4. How do you score progress on a key result?
5. What does a flattening retention curve mean?
6. Why add a guardrail to a North Star?
7. Why decide the sample size before an experiment?

Common mistakes:

- Writing risks as vague worries with no trigger or owner.
- Key results that are tasks.
- Celebrating overall user growth while retention falls.
- Stopping an experiment the moment it looks significant.
- Using effort tracking to police people.

## Level 4: Expert and professional judgement

### What you will be able to do

- You can predict how a metric will be gamed and design the report so it is hard to game.
- You can write a status report that is honest when the news is bad.
- You can review someone else's plan and ask the questions that matter.
- You can run a small portfolio: decide what to start, continue, and stop.
- You can handle ethics and stakeholder conflict without losing trust.
- You can set the practices of a team and know when to remove them.

### Core ideas

#### Goodhart's law and how metrics get gamed

**When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.** Velocity is a team's private capacity gauge; make it a target and points inflate. Burndown becomes a target and cards get closed half-done. "Bugs closed" becomes a target and people split one bug into five. Cycle time becomes a target and people avoid starting hard items. Defences: report several measures that pull against each other (speed and quality), use them to ask questions rather than to rank people, change the measure when it starts to bend behaviour, and never compare teams' velocities. When a number looks wonderful, ask what behaviour produced it.

#### Honest reporting

Status colours drift green until suddenly red: the "watermelon" project is green outside and red inside. Make green mean something checkable ("forecast inside the committed range; no top risk above threshold") and give each colour a rule. A good report fits on one page: where we are against the plan (EV, forecast range), what changed since last time, the top three risks with owners, the decision or help needed and by when. Report bad news early and with a proposal: "CPI is 0.76; at this rate the project finishes about 30% over; here are three options with their costs." Never bury the forecast in a footnote. If you do not know, say you do not know and say when you will.

#### Reviewing someone else's plan

Questions, in order: What problem does this solve and how will we know? Who decided this was the priority, and what was left out? What is the critical path and what is its float? Which estimates are guesses, and what is the range? What are the top risks, who owns them, and what is the trigger? What is the smallest slice that gives real feedback? What happens if the key person is away for two weeks? Where is the plan's assumption that customers will behave a certain way, and how cheaply can we test it? Write reviews as questions and evidence, and name what would change your mind.

#### Portfolio decisions: start, continue, stop

With several projects and limited people, the largest error is starting too much: WIP applies to portfolios as much as to boards. Rank candidate work by a consistent method (RICE or WSJF), look at capacity honestly, and decide what **stops**. Beware the **sunk cost fallacy**: money and months already spent are gone and should not decide whether to continue; only the cost to finish and the expected value should. A project with a TCPI far above anything the team has ever achieved is a candidate to stop or re-scope, and saying so early is a service. Keep a decision log: what was decided, by whom, on what evidence, and what would reverse it.

#### Ethics, conflict and stakeholders

You will be pressured to report better numbers, to promise dates you do not believe, or to cut testing. Hold a line: you can disagree and commit to a decision, but you do not misreport. Escalate openly, in writing, with options. Map stakeholders by influence and interest, and talk to those with high influence early, even when it is uncomfortable. When two sponsors want opposite things, do not choose in secret; put the trade-off in front of both, with the numbers. Respect users: do not run experiments that deceive or harm them, and be careful with personal data you collect for analytics (collect the minimum and check the rules that apply where you operate). Credit the team in public; take the blame for the plan in private and in public.

#### Running a team's practices

Start small: a board with limits, a short weekly planning, a retrospective each sprint or two that produces one change, a risk review, and a one-page report. Each practice should have a purpose you can state; if nobody can, remove it. Revisit your estimates against actuals each quarter. Avoid ceremony for its own sake: a ten-person standup that reads statuses aloud is a meeting, not coordination. Adapt to scale: a one-person project needs a list and a calendar, a twenty-team programme needs shared definitions, dependency tracking and a regular portfolio review. Use the lightest process that keeps surprises rare.

### Practice on your own

1. Pick three common metrics and write, for each, how you would game it and what balancing measure catches that.
2. Write a one-page status report for your running project that has a bad number in it. Show it to a friend and ask whether they understand what you need from them.
3. Review a real plan (a friend's, an open-source roadmap) with the questions above and write ten of your own.
4. List five candidate projects. Score with RICE, state capacity, and choose what to start, what to continue and what to stop. Write the decision log entry.
5. Run a retrospective on a recent project of yours and extract one concrete change you will try.
6. Draw a stakeholder map for a real situation and plan the first conversation with the most influential person.

### Exercises

1. A team is told to raise velocity 20%. Predict two things that happen and a better target.
2. A project has spent 80% of the budget and is 50% complete by earned value. A sponsor says "we have come too far to stop". Reply.
3. Rewrite "Project is on track, some minor risks" as a checkable statement for a project with CPI 0.85 and SPI 0.9.
4. A sponsor wants you to leave a known risk off the report to avoid alarming the board. Reply.
5. Write five questions you would ask in a plan review that a typical Gantt chart cannot answer.
6. Design the weekly one-page report for a six-person team: five headings and the metric under each.

### Answers and hints

1. Points inflate and stories are sliced to look big; teams may skip quality work. Better: a target on a customer outcome plus a guardrail on defects, and use velocity only for forecasting.
2. Hint: BAC 100, spent 80, earned 50: CPI = 50 / 80 = 0.625; EAC = 100 / 0.625 = 160. The sunk 80 is gone; ask whether the remaining 80 to finish (EAC minus spent) buys more value than the best alternative use of that 80.
3. "Spending 15% over plan for the work done and about 10% behind schedule; forecast 118% of budget (EAC = BAC / 0.85); the top risk is X, owned by Y, decision needed by date Z."
4. Do not omit it. Offer to present it with the mitigation and the cost, so the board sees you are in control; if still asked, escalate in writing.

### Checkpoint

Answer aloud:

1. What is Goodhart's law, with an example from this part?
2. What makes a status colour trustworthy?
3. What belongs on a one-page report?
4. What is the sunk cost fallacy and how does it appear in a project?
5. Name five questions for reviewing a plan.
6. What do you do when asked to misreport?
7. How do you decide which practices to drop?

Common mistakes:

- Turning a diagnostic measure into a target.
- Reporting green until the last minute.
- Continuing a project because of money already spent.
- Burying bad news in detail.
- Adding process faster than you remove it.

## Capstone projects

### Capstone 1: A one-page product brief and prioritised backlog (small, about 6 hours)

**Brief.** Choose a real problem for a small group of people (your family, a club, a team). Write a one-page PRD and a prioritised backlog of at least 12 items.

**Deliverables.** The PRD with a measurable success criterion and an explicit non-goal; a backlog scored with RICE and WSJF with the two rankings compared; a MoSCoW split with the Must share; a one-paragraph note on where you overrode the scores and why.

**Rubric** (0 to 2 each; 8 or more is good). Problem stated before solution; success is testable; scores are computed correctly; overrides are explained honestly.

### Capstone 2: Plan, forecast and report a small project (medium, about 15 hours)

**Brief.** Plan and run a real project of two to four weeks (a move, an event, a small build). Track it on a board.

**Deliverables.** A board with start and finish dates, a dependency network with the critical path and float, a range forecast updated weekly, earned value at three dates, a risk register with at least six risks, and three weekly one-page reports. Finish with a retrospective.

**Rubric** (0 to 2 each; 10 or more is good). The forecast is a range and changes as evidence arrives; the critical path is right; earned value uses actual costs; the reports state bad news plainly; the retrospective produces a change.

### Capstone 3: A measured product experiment (large, about 30 hours)

**Brief.** Launch something small to real people (a landing page, a newsletter, a tool for a club) with a North Star, objectives and an experiment.

**Deliverables.** An objective with three key results; a North Star with two input metrics and a guardrail; a funnel and a retention cohort table from real data; one pre-planned A/B test with sample size and decision rule written before you start; a decision log; a final report stating what worked, what did not, and what you would stop.

**Rubric** (0 to 2 each; 12 or more is good). Key results are outcomes; data is real and definitions are written; the experiment is planned before it runs; the conclusion matches the strength of evidence; at least one thing is honestly stopped or changed.

## Tools reference

| Tool | What it is for | When you outgrow it |
|---|---|---|
| Paper board or sticky notes | Seeing flow with a small team | When people are remote or you need history |
| A spreadsheet | RICE, WSJF, velocity, EV, risk register, funnels | When many people edit at once or you need dependencies |
| A board tool (any issue tracker) | Shared cards, statuses, dates, simple reports | When you need portfolio roll-ups or resource planning |
| A scheduling tool or script | Critical path and float on larger networks | When you need resource levelling and baselines |
| A product analytics tool | Funnels, cohorts, event data | When you need warehouse-level analysis (see Part 1 and Part 4) |
| An experimentation tool or a stats library | Randomisation and significance tests | When you run many concurrent tests |
| A document or wiki | PRDs, decision logs, retrospectives | Keep it; add templates and review habits |

## Glossary

- **PRD**: product requirements document; the problem, measure, scope and non-scope on one page.
- **Scope creep**: unplanned additions to scope, usually small and cumulative.
- **Epic / story / task**: a large item split later / a user-visible slice of value / a unit of work.
- **Milestone**: a point in time with no duration of its own.
- **Backlog**: the ordered list of work not yet committed.
- **Sprint**: a fixed, short period (often one or two weeks) that ends with finished work.
- **RICE**: Reach x Impact x Confidence / Effort.
- **WSJF**: weighted shortest job first; (value + time criticality + risk reduction) / size.
- **MoSCoW**: Must, Should, Could, Won't (this time).
- **Velocity**: story points completed per sprint.
- **Burndown**: remaining work plotted against time and an ideal line.
- **Cycle time / lead time**: started to done / created to done.
- **Throughput**: items finished per unit of time.
- **WIP**: work in progress; limit it to finish things.
- **Little's law**: average WIP = throughput x average cycle time.
- **Critical path**: the chain of dependent items with zero float.
- **Float**: how long an item can slip without delaying the project.
- **PV, EV, AC**: planned value, earned value, actual cost.
- **CPI, SPI**: cost and schedule performance indexes (EV / AC, EV / PV).
- **EAC, TCPI**: estimate at completion; to-complete performance index.
- **Risk exposure / EMV**: probability x impact / probability x cost.
- **OKR**: objective and key results.
- **Funnel / cohort / retention**: step-by-step conversion / users grouped by start date / share who return.
- **North Star metric**: the single measure that best captures delivered customer value.
- **Guardrail metric**: a measure that must not worsen while chasing another.
- **Goodhart's law**: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure.
- **Sunk cost**: money or time already spent and unrecoverable; it should not drive the next decision.

## Career notes

**Roles and titles.** Project coordinator, project manager, scrum master or delivery lead, product owner, product manager, programme manager, business analyst (see Part 7), operations manager, chief of staff. The overlap with a systems analyst is large; the overlap with data roles grows as product work becomes more measurement-driven.

**How to show your skill.** Publish the three capstones with the PRD, board history, forecasts, reports and decision log, including the places you were wrong. Describe one project you stopped or reshaped on evidence. Hiring managers look for judgement under uncertainty: how you set priorities, how you told bad news, what you left out.

**What to learn next.** Statistics for experiments (see Part 2), SQL for your own product analytics (see Part 1), basic finance (budgets, cash flow, return on investment), facilitation and negotiation, user research methods, and how engineering teams work (see Part 8). Project and product bodies publish certifications and guides; they change over time, so check the current exam objectives before you invest, and treat a certificate as supporting evidence, never a substitute for a record of projects you have delivered.

## Match it in the app

This book works without the dashboard. If you have it, the **Project & Product** track (Tracks, then Project & Product) is a working board, not a picture: you add items, sprints, risks and key results and every number is computed from them. In the **Practice** workspace press **Load example project** (it works only in Practice and only when the board is empty) to get 3 sprints, 12 items, 4 risks and 3 key results. The **Real** workspace starts empty, for your own project; do not mix the two. The panel has twelve tabs: Board, Prioritisation, Sprints, Flow, Schedule, Earned value, Risks, OKRs, Product analytics, Time, Calendar and Automations. The vocabulary page is `docs/PROJECT_PRODUCT_MANAGEMENT.md`, the calculations are in `backend/app/services/pm.py`, and every formula is checked by hand in `backend/tests/test_pm.py`; reading those tests is excellent practice.

**Level 1 (foundations).** On **Board**, note the five columns (backlog, todo, doing, review, done) and the item kinds; moving a card to doing stamps a start date and moving it to done stamps the finish. On **Prioritisation**, "Backups and restore" has reach 100, impact 2, confidence 0.9, effort 1, so RICE 100 x 2 x 0.9 / 1 = 180; check the table agrees. Only items with all four WSJF inputs get a WSJF score. The MoSCoW summary shows the Must share; in the example it is about 43% by points (36 of 83), under the 60% guide. **Sprints** shows velocity history and the burndown: Sprints 1 and 2 each committed and finished 13 points.

**Level 2 (practitioner).** **Flow** shows cycle and lead time with median and 85th percentile, weekly throughput, a cumulative flow diagram, and actual WIP beside what Little's law expects. **Sprints** forecasts from the last three finished sprints: the example has 23 points left in stories, tasks and bugs, so about 1.8 sprints (23 / 13); epics and milestones are not counted. **Schedule** computes ES, EF, LS, LF and float and refuses a dependency loop; the example critical path runs through design, import, the KPI dashboard, workspaces, scheduled refresh and the release milestone, 28 days, while "Chart gallery" has 8 days of float. The finish date is computed from planned starts and ignores progress, so it is already in the past, which is the limit noted in the text. **Earned value**: BAC 27,000, EV 15,000, AC 19,800, so CPI is about 0.76.

**Level 3 (advanced).** **Risks** takes probability (0 to 1) and a dollar impact, sums EMV for open risks as a reserve hint (the example's four risks: 2,400 + 750 + 600 + 7,200 = 10,950) and places each on a 3 x 3 grid; note this differs from the 1 to 5 scoring in the text, which is the same idea on another scale. **OKRs** scores (current - start) / (target - start), capped 0 to 100%: the example key results are at 80%, about 84% and 100%. **Product analytics** computes from your own data, treating entities as accounts: weekly actives, DAU/MAU stickiness, a status funnel, cohort retention by first month and a North Star chosen in the KPI builder. **Time** logs hours; once hours exist earned value uses hours x rate as actual cost. **Automations** holds simple if-this-then-that rules with a preview before you run them; they touch only items in the app. The track's project-and-product agent can draft items, risks and key results as cards you approve; it cannot change anything by itself.

**Level 4 (expert).** The app gives you the evidence for judgement exercises: read the Earned value "reading line" and write the one-page report from the text; look at Flow and decide which metric you would never turn into a target. Practise the review questions on the example project and write your decision log entries in your own notes.

**What the app does not cover.** There is no dedicated roadmap view: use Schedule, Calendar and Sprints together, or draw a roadmap yourself. It has no resource levelling or baselines, no dependency types beyond finish-to-start, no stakeholder map, status-colour rules, retrospective or decision log, no experiment or significance tool for A/B tests, and no people management. Its analytics stand in "entities" for users, so treat the product tab as a way to learn funnels and cohorts, not as real product telemetry. It is a personal-scale tracker, and the example project is invented.
