# Welcome {-}

This book teaches eleven disciplines of the data, software and infrastructure world, each from first principles to professional judgement. Every part stands alone, uses free tools, and works with nothing but a computer, a notebook and some patience. The disciplines are:

| Part | Discipline | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Data Analyst | Turn data into decisions people can act on |
| 2 | Data Scientist | Find out what is true and what will happen, honestly |
| 3 | Machine Learning | Build and evaluate models that survive contact with reality |
| 4 | Data Engineering | Move data reliably from where it is made to where it is used |
| 5 | AI Engineering | Build dependable products on top of language models |
| 6 | Project and Product | Decide what to build, in what order, on time and on budget |
| 7 | Systems Analyst | Work out what a system must do and prove it does |
| 8 | Full Stack Developer | Own a feature from the table to the screen |
| 9 | Cybersecurity | Protect systems you own: find weaknesses, read logs, respond |
| 10 | Network Engineer | Design, build and fix the paths data takes |
| 11 | IT Specialist | Keep people productive, protect data, know what you own |

## How every part is built

Each part follows the same shape so you always know where you are.

* **The role in one page** tells you what the work is, what it is not, and what is hard about it.
* **Four levels.** Level 1 assumes nothing. Level 2 makes you a working practitioner. Level 3 covers the techniques that separate good from adequate. Level 4 is about judgement: trade-offs, failure modes, reviewing other people's work, honesty, and working at team scale.
* **Each level** lists what you will be able to do, teaches the core ideas through worked examples, gives practice you can do on your own, sets exercises with answers and hints for many of them, and ends with a checkpoint and a list of common mistakes.
* **Capstone projects** (three per part, small to large) let you prove the skill to yourself and to others.
* **Tools reference, glossary and career notes** close each part.
* **Match it in the app** is the last section of each part. It maps the levels to the companion dashboard for readers who use it. The book is complete without it.

## How to study

**Pace.** The time estimates in each part assume about five hours a week. A level is typically three to eight weeks. Do not rush Level 1; every later level stands on it.

**Learn by producing.** Reading feels like learning but is not. After each concept, close the book and reproduce the worked example from memory, then change one number and predict the result before running it. The exercises exist for this.

**Say it aloud.** The checkpoint questions are meant to be answered out loud, without notes. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not yet own it. Return to the concept and try again; this is the normal path, not failure.

**Keep a lab notebook.** For every exercise write the question, what you tried, what happened and what you learned. Mistakes written down are the most valuable pages you will keep.

**Spaced review.** Revisit a finished level after one week, one month and three months. Re-do three exercises you found hard. Forgetting then re-learning is what makes knowledge last.

**Only practise on what you own.** Anything involving networks, accounts or security tools must be done on your own equipment, in a lab you built, or with written permission. This rule is absolute and is repeated where it matters.

## Choosing an order

The disciplines overlap. The suggested routes below share ideas so that effort carries across.

| Goal | Suggested order |
|---|---|
| Work with data | 1, 2, 3, then 4 and 5 |
| Build software | 8, 7, 4, then 9 |
| Run and secure infrastructure | 11, 10, 9, then 8 |
| Lead and organise work | 7, 6, 1 |
| Not sure yet | 1 (it is the best general foundation), then 11 and 8 to sample the infrastructure and software worlds |

Shared foundations worth knowing: **SQL** (Parts 1, 2, 4, 7, 8), **statistics** (Parts 1, 2, 3), **the command line and Git** (Parts 4, 8, 9, 10, 11), **networking basics** (Parts 8, 9, 10, 11), and **clear writing** (every part). A reader who masters writing a clear one-page memo will outperform one who knows more tools.

## A note on honesty

Techniques in this book are taught with their limits attached: a correlation is not a cause; a detection finding is a lead and not a verdict; RAID is not a backup; a model that cannot beat a simple baseline is a bug; a guardrail is not a vault. Tools and versions change quickly. Where this book names a product, treat the name as an example of a category, and check current documentation before you rely on a menu path, a price, a command flag or an exam objective.
