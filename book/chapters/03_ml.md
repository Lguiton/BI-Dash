# Part 3: Machine Learning

## The role in one page

A machine learning practitioner builds models that make predictions, and then does the less glamorous part of the job: proving that those predictions are good enough to use, and noticing when they stop being so. The fitting step takes seconds. Most of the work is deciding what to predict, which information the model may legitimately use, how to test it honestly, and what to do when the world changes.

You will work with data analysts and scientists, who frame the question; data engineers, who supply fresh tables; and the people who use the prediction, such as an operations manager or a finance lead. Their view of the cost of a wrong answer matters more than any score.

A typical day looks like this. In the morning you check whether yesterday's model still behaves, by looking at monitoring and drift reports. Then you spend a couple of hours on one experiment: a new feature, a different split, a threshold change. You write down what you tried and what happened, including the failures. In the afternoon you review a colleague's model, usually by asking where the leakage is. You finish by writing a short, plain note for a non-technical reader: what the model does, how well, and where it should not be trusted.

Good looks like this. Every model is compared with a naive baseline. Splits respect time. The metric matches the cost of mistakes. Experiments are recorded so that anyone can reproduce them. Claims come with uncertainty. When a model is retired, someone decided that deliberately.

What it is not: it is not collecting algorithm names, and it is not leaderboard chasing. A gradient boosting model that loses to "predict the average" is a bug, not a result. It is also not the same as AI engineering (Part 5), which wraps existing models in applications, and not the same as data science (Part 2), which leans on statistics and explanation more than on deployment.

The hard parts, honestly:

- Most of your time goes to data problems, not modelling.
- Honest evaluation is boring and often delivers bad news. A model that looks 95 percent accurate may catch nothing.
- Many problems do not need machine learning. A rule or a simple average sometimes wins, and saying so is part of the job.
- Real outcomes arrive late. You often cannot tell for weeks whether a prediction was right.
- Nobody notices a model that quietly decays. You have to build the alarm yourself.

Pair reading with doing. Each level has a "Practice on your own" section of labs that need only a spreadsheet or a scripting language with a machine learning library (Python with scikit-learn and pandas is a common choice, but R or any equivalent works). A final optional section, "Match it in the app", maps each level to a companion application.

Say the checkpoint answers aloud. If you cannot explain an idea without looking, you do not own it yet.

Two things to keep in mind throughout. Scores from practice data are about that data, not a general truth. And a result from one time split is a single reading, not a verdict.

## Level 1: Foundations (beginner)

### What you will be able to do

- You can explain what a model, a feature and a target are, in one sentence each.
- You can say whether a task is regression or classification.
- You can compute a baseline and say whether a model beats it.
- You can split data by time and explain why a shuffled split flatters a model.
- You can compute MAE, RMSE and R² by hand on four numbers.
- You can name one feature that would leak the answer and say why.

### Core ideas

#### A model is a rule learned from past examples, and it must beat a baseline

A model is a rule fitted to examples: given the inputs (features), produce a guess for the answer (the target). Before you trust one, build the dullest possible rival. For a number, the rival is "always predict the training average". For a yes or no, it is "always predict the most common answer". Call this the baseline, and show it next to every score.

Worked example. You are predicting revenue for four test records: 100, 120, 80 and 200. The training average was 110, so the baseline predicts 110 each time. Its absolute errors are 10, 10, 30 and 90, which average 35. A model predicts 105, 115, 90 and 170. Its errors are 5, 5, 10 and 30, which average 12.5. The model's error is about a third of the baseline's, so it has learned something. If it had scored 36, it would have lost to a constant, and you would drop it.

The baseline also tells you what "good" means. An error of 12.5 sounds abstract until you see that doing nothing costs 35.

#### Split by time, never at random

Your model will be used on days it has not seen. So test it on days it has not seen. Train on the earlier days and test on the most recent ones. A random shuffle lets the model see Tuesday's and Thursday's records while predicting Wednesday's. Neighbouring days resemble each other, so it looks better than it will in use.

Split on a date boundary. With a test size of 20 percent, it takes the number of distinct days, multiplies by 0.2 and rounds. On a year of 365 days that is 73 test days, and everything before the cutoff date trains the model. No single day ever sits on both sides.

Worked example. Ten days of data, test size 20 percent: round(10 × 0.2) = 2 test days. If the days are 1 to 10, the cutoff is day 9. Days 1 to 8 train, days 9 and 10 test. Refuse to run with fewer than four distinct days; and be wary of fitting anything on under 100 rows.

#### Features are what the model may look at, and the question is "will I know this beforehand?"

The target is the thing you predict. Features are the columns you give the model. Each feature must pass one test: will I actually know this at the moment I need the prediction? If you want to predict a record's revenue before it happens, you cannot use anything recorded after.

Imagine a table of operations records with columns such as category, weekday, month, units processed, duration, operational cost and a unit ID. The ID identifies the unit, so a flexible model can memorise it instead of learning a pattern. Units processed and duration move almost one to one with revenue; that is fine if you know them in advance, and leakage if you will not. Predicting profit while feeding in operational cost is nearly an algebra problem, because profit is revenue minus cost.

Worked example. You want to predict revenue before a job starts. Units planned is a legitimate feature. Operational cost is recorded at the end, and it is driven by the work, so it quietly carries part of the answer. A score that looks wonderful because of a feature like that is fiction.

#### Regression error: MAE, RMSE and R²

For numbers you will see three scores. MAE is the average absolute miss, in the same units as the target. RMSE squares the misses, averages them, then takes the square root, so it punishes big misses more. R² compares your squared errors with those of always predicting the test-set average: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than that average, and negative is worse.

Worked example, using the same four records. The test average is 125. The total squared distance of the actuals from 125 is 625 + 25 + 2,025 + 5,625 = 8,300. The model's squared errors are 25 + 25 + 100 + 900 = 1,050. So R² = 1 − 1,050 / 8,300 = 0.873, and RMSE = √(1,050 / 4) = 16.2, against an MAE of 12.5. RMSE is bigger than MAE because one record missed by 30. If you measure the baseline's R² against the test average it is exactly 0. If you predict the training average instead, it can come out negative: here it would be −0.108.

### Practice on your own

Build your own practice data so the labs run anywhere. In a spreadsheet or a script, create 120 days of daily records for 5 units. Give each record units processed (random from 20 to 100), a weekday effect, and revenue of about 5 times units plus noise of plus or minus 40. Sort by date.

1. Split by date: the first 96 days train, the last 24 test. Compute the training average revenue and the baseline MAE on the test days.
2. Fit a straight line of revenue on units, using a spreadsheet trendline or any library. Compute its MAE and R² on the test days. Does it beat the baseline?
3. Repeat with a shuffled split. Note how the score changes, and write down why it is not a fair test.
4. Add a column that is revenue minus a small noise term and call it "adjusted revenue". Use it as a feature, watch R² jump, and explain why that is leakage.

### Exercises

1. Test values are 10, 20, 30. The training average is 15. Compute the baseline MAE.
2. A model predicts 12, 18, 33 for those values. Compute its MAE. Does it beat the baseline?
3. Give a classification and a regression question from your own life.
4. You have 50 days of data and a test size of 20 percent. How many test days?
5. A colleague shuffles rows before splitting. Explain the problem in two sentences.
6. List three features for predicting next week's delivery count and say which one you would not know in advance.
7. For exercise 2, compute RMSE and say why it is larger than MAE.
8. A model has R² of 0.99 on a task where nobody expected it to be easy. What is your first question?

### Answers and hints

1. Errors are 5, 5, 15 and the mean is 25 / 3 = 8.33.
2. Errors are 2, 2, 3 and the mean is 7 / 3 = 2.33. It beats the baseline easily.
4. round(50 × 0.2) = 10 days.
5. A shuffle puts neighbouring days on both sides, so the model has effectively seen the answer's neighbours. Real use is always on unseen future days, so the score is too optimistic.
7. Squares are 4, 4, 9; the mean is 5.67 and the root is 2.38. It is larger because the 3 is penalised more than the 2s.
8. "Is one of my features a copy of the target, or something I will not know in time?"

Say aloud:

1. What is a baseline, and what is the baseline for a yes or no question?
2. Why is the split by date and not shuffled?
3. What does an R² of 0 mean? Of a negative number?
4. Why is RMSE never smaller than MAE?
5. What question do you ask of every feature?
6. Why be wary of fitting a model on under 100 rows?
7. Why is a "great" score on one run not yet a result?

Common mistakes:

- Reporting a score without the baseline next to it.
- Splitting randomly because it is the default in a tutorial.
- Treating R² as a percentage of correct predictions.
- Using a feature only known after the event.

## Level 2: Practitioner (intermediate)

### What you will be able to do

- You can build a pipeline that learns preprocessing from training data only.
- You can read a confusion matrix and compute precision, recall and F1 from it.
- You can show why accuracy misleads when the event is rare.
- You can choose a decision threshold from costs, and defend it.
- You can explain what permutation importance and time-ordered cross-validation tell you.
- You can compare several models and say when a gap is just noise.

### Core ideas

#### Pipelines keep preprocessing honest

Models need numbers on comparable scales, with no gaps. A pipeline bundles the preprocessing and the model so both are fitted on the training rows only. A common recipe fills missing numbers with the median, standardises them, and one-hot encodes text columns. The key rule is that the scaler's mean and spread come from training data. If you compute them on everything, test information leaks into training.

Worked example. Training values for duration are 10, 20 and 30. The mean is 20. The standard deviation, computed the way scikit-learn's StandardScaler does it (dividing by n), is √(200 / 3) = 8.165. So 10 becomes −1.22, 20 becomes 0 and 30 becomes +1.22. A test value of 40 becomes (40 − 20) / 8.165 = 2.45, using the training numbers, not its own. One-hot encoding turns a three-valued column such as category into three columns of 0 and 1. A category never seen in training becomes all zeros rather than a crash, if you set the encoder to ignore unknown values.

#### Accuracy lies when the event is rare

Suppose about 1 record in 14 ends up Delayed or Cancelled. A model that always says "Completed" is right about 93 percent of the time and catches nothing. The confusion matrix counts four outcomes: true negatives, false positives (false alarms), false negatives (misses) and true positives.

Worked example. 1,000 test records, 70 real problems. A model flags 70 records: 28 are real (TP = 28), 42 are not (FP = 42). It misses 42 (FN = 42), and TN = 930 − 42 = 888.

- Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 28 / 70 = 0.40.
- Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 28 / 70 = 0.40.
- F1, the harmonic mean, is 0.40.
- Accuracy = (28 + 888) / 1,000 = 91.6 percent.

That accuracy is lower than the 93 percent you get from always saying "Completed", yet the model catches 28 problems and the constant catches none. That is the lesson: when accuracy fails to beat the baseline, accuracy is the wrong yardstick.

#### The threshold is a business decision

A classifier outputs a probability. The threshold turns it into a flag. Lowering it catches more problems and raises false alarms. There is no free setting, only a trade-off, and its right position depends on what each mistake costs.

Worked example. Using the same 1,000 records, suppose a missed problem costs 200 dollars and a false alarm costs 20. At threshold 0.5 the model above has 42 misses and 42 false alarms: 42 × 200 + 42 × 20 = 9,240 dollars. Lower the threshold and you get TP = 50, FP = 150, FN = 20: precision 0.25, recall 0.71, F1 0.37, accuracy 83 percent. The cost is 20 × 200 + 150 × 20 = 7,000 dollars. F1 fell, accuracy fell, and the cost fell by about 2,240 dollars. "Flag nothing" would cost 70 × 200 = 14,000. The best threshold is the one that minimises cost, which F1 does not know about.

#### Balancing classes shifts the model, not the world

Class weights tell the model to treat the rare class as more important. With 7 percent positives, scikit-learn's "balanced" setting weights each positive at 1 / (2 × 0.07) = 7.14 and each negative at 1 / (2 × 0.93) = 0.54. The model then spends more effort on the rare class, so recall rises and precision falls. It is similar in spirit to lowering the threshold, but it changes the fitted model, not just the cut-off.

Neither is magic. Turn weighting on and watch recall climb while precision drops. Some model types, such as small neural networks in common libraries, have no class-weight option; use the threshold there.

#### Importance and stability

Permutation importance shuffles one feature in the test set and measures how much the score drops. If R² falls from 0.80 to 0.35 after shuffling units processed, that feature's importance is 0.45. A feature near zero adds nothing. Correlated features share credit: units and duration move together, so either may come out on top and the other looks weak.

Time-ordered cross-validation checks stability. With 100 ordered rows and four splits, scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit tests on rows 20 to 39 after training on 0 to 19, then 40 to 59 after 0 to 39, and so on. Run it on the training period only. If the four scores swing widely, one test score is not to be trusted.

#### Compare models on the same split, and respect ties

Train every candidate on one split and rank them. Treat two models within about 0.02 on the main score as a tie, and prefer the simpler or faster one. One split is a single sample of the future.

### Practice on your own

Extend your Level 1 data with a problem flag: make about 7 percent of records "problem" records, with slightly higher odds when duration is long. Keep the same time split.

1. Fit a logistic regression on units, duration and category. Compute accuracy, precision, recall and F1, and compare accuracy with "always predict no problem".
2. Print the confusion matrix at thresholds 0.5, 0.3, 0.15 and 0.1. Record caught problems and false alarms for each.
3. Refit with balanced class weights and compare recall and precision at 0.5.
4. Fit three model types (for example logistic regression, a random forest and gradient boosting) on one split, and rank them. Say whether any gap is a tie.

### Exercises

1. Compute accuracy, precision and recall for TP 10, FP 30, FN 10, TN 950.
2. What accuracy does "always predict no problem" get on that data?
3. Explain why the scaler is fitted on training rows only.
4. A missed fraud costs 500 dollars and a false alarm costs 5. Which way should the threshold move, and why?
5. Using costs of 100 for a miss and 10 for a false alarm: threshold A gives FN 30, FP 20; threshold B gives FN 10, FP 90. Which is cheaper?
6. Compute the balanced class weights for 2 percent positives.
7. Two models score R² 0.81 and 0.80 on one split. What do you recommend?
8. Why might permutation importance understate a feature?

### Answers and hints

1. Accuracy = 960 / 1,000 = 0.96. Precision = 10 / 40 = 0.25. Recall = 10 / 20 = 0.50.
2. 980 of 1,000 are negatives, so 98 percent, which beats the model's 96 percent.
4. Lower it: a miss is 100 times dearer, so accept many more false alarms.
5. A: 30 × 100 + 20 × 10 = 3,200. B: 10 × 100 + 90 × 10 = 1,900. B is cheaper.
6. Positive: 1 / (2 × 0.02) = 25. Negative: 1 / (2 × 0.98) = 0.51.
7. It is a tie, because the gap is under 0.02. Prefer the simpler or faster model, and test on another period.
8. A correlated feature carries the same information, so shuffling one still leaves the other to cover for it.

Common mistakes:

- Tuning the threshold on the test set (more on this in Level 3).
- Reporting F1 without saying what a miss costs.
- Scaling or imputing on the whole dataset before splitting.
- Reading importance as cause and effect.
- Crowning a winner from a 0.01 difference.

## Level 3: Advanced

### What you will be able to do

- You can tune a threshold on a validation split and report once on the test set.
- You can put a confidence range around a score and say what it means for a decision.
- You can log, compare and reproduce experiments in a tracker and in MLflow.
- You can register a model version, move it through stages and explain what each stage promises.
- You can compute PSI by hand and decide what a drift reading means.
- You can find at least three kinds of leakage in someone else's pipeline.

### Core ideas

#### Tune on validation, report once on test

If you choose the threshold by looking at the test set, you have tuned to the exam, and the test score is no longer an honest estimate. The fix is a third slice. Split the training period again, in time order: fit on the earlier part, choose the threshold on the later part (validation), then fit as normal and report on the test set exactly once. A reasonable recipe is to cut at the 75 percent point of the training days, sweep thresholds from 0.02 to 0.48, and take the highest F1 on validation, with the smallest threshold winning a tie.

Worked example. You sweep thresholds on validation and get F1 of 0.31 at 0.10, 0.36 at 0.14 and 0.34 at 0.20. You pick 0.14. On test it gives F1 0.33. That is a believable number, a little lower than the 0.36 you saw on validation. If you had swept on the test set you would have reported 0.36 and been quietly wrong. The drop from validation to test is normal and is itself information.

#### Small test sets mean wide error bars

About 1 in 14 records is a problem. With a 20 percent test window you may have only a few dozen real problems, and every recall figure rests on them. A rough 95 percent range for a proportion p measured on n cases is p ± 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) / n).

Worked example. Recall is 28 of 70, so p = 0.40. The standard error is √(0.4 × 0.6 / 70) = 0.0586, and 1.96 times that is 0.115. So the honest statement is "recall about 0.40, plausibly between 0.29 and 0.52". Another model with recall 0.45 is not clearly better. This is a rough normal approximation, and it is poor for very small counts, but it stops you from over-reading a gap of a few points.

#### Track every experiment

After a dozen runs nobody remembers which settings gave which score. A tracker records, for each run, the parameters, the metrics and the fitted model. Even a spreadsheet or CSV file with one row per run works. MLflow is a real industry tool that does the same: log_param records what you chose, log_metric what you got, log_artifact the file, and search_runs returns a table sorted however you like.

Worked example. Three runs on the same split: ridge MAE 24.1, random forest 19.8, gradient boosting 19.5. A tracker lets you answer, a month later, what features and test size produced 19.5. Note the data and code version too; a score you cannot reproduce is a rumour.

#### The registry gives a model a name, a version and a stage

A registry stores each trained model with its metrics and a profile of its training data under a name and version number (revenue_model v1, v2 and so on). A new version must predict the same task as earlier ones; a different task needs a new name. A version moves through stages: none, staging, production, archived. Only one version per name is in production, and promoting another archives the old one.

The stage is a promise about process, not about quality. Production means a person decided to trust this version. The model card lists its test score against the baseline and its caveats. Serving takes input rows and returns estimates, and should warn if a value is outside the range the model saw. The models are joblib files on disk, so load only a folder you made yourself: loading a pickled file can run code.

#### Drift: the inputs changed

A model is trained on one slice of the world. If the inputs shift, its error can grow without anything breaking. A drift check compares the training profile with the most recent rows (for example the last 14 days, and at least 30 rows), one feature at a time, using the population stability index:

PSI = sum over bins of (actual share − expected share) × ln(actual share / expected share)

A common recipe bins training values into ten quantile groups for numbers, and uses the most common values plus an "other" bucket for text. Under 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 some drift, over 0.25 significant.

Worked example, with five bins of 20 percent each at training. Recent shares are 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 percent. The terms are (−0.10 × ln 0.5) = 0.0693, (−0.05 × ln 0.75) = 0.0144, 0, (0.05 × ln 1.25) = 0.0112 and (0.10 × ln 1.5) = 0.0405. The total is 0.135: some drift. Shares of 5, 10, 20, 30 and 35 percent give 0.402: significant.

Drift looks at inputs only. It cannot say the model is wrong, and a stable PSI does not prove it is right. Keep every reading, so you can chart drift over time.

#### Leakage has several faces

Beyond "a feature that copies the target" there are others. Target leakage: a feature computed after the event, such as cost for a revenue prediction. Temporal leakage: shuffled splits, or a rolling average that includes future days. Preprocessing leakage: scaling or imputing before the split. Identity leakage: an ID-like feature, such as a unit or zone code, lets a flexible model memorise each unit. Selection leakage: choosing features or thresholds using the test set.

2. The smallest, 0.10.
4. (−0.10 × ln 0.8) + (0.10 × ln 1.2) = 0.0223 + 0.0182 = 0.0405. Stable.

Common mistakes:

- Reporting the best of many test-set tries as if it were one.
- Promoting a version to production on a one-split win.
- Reading low PSI as proof the model is right.
- Treating ID columns as harmless features.

## Level 4: Expert and professional judgement

### What you will be able to do

- You can choose between a simple and a complex model on cost, risk and upkeep, not only score.
- You can name the main ways a deployed model fails silently, and the monitor for each.
- You can review someone else's model and produce a short list of findings ranked by risk.
- You can write about a model honestly, including uncertainty and where it must not be used.
- You can say when machine learning is the wrong tool.
- You can set up ownership, review and retraining habits for a team.

### Core ideas

#### Trade-offs: the best score is rarely the best model

A model has a running cost: compute, monitoring, retraining, explanation, and people who understand it. Weigh the gain against that cost in the units the business uses.

Worked example. Predicting problem records, a rule ("flag records whose duration is over the 90th percentile") achieves 40 of 70 problems caught with 100 false alarms. A boosted model catches 50 with 150 false alarms. With a miss at 200 dollars and an alarm at 20: the rule costs 30 × 200 + 100 × 20 = 8,000; the model costs 20 × 200 + 150 × 20 = 7,000. The model saves 1,000 dollars per 1,000 records, but if the extra upkeep is worth more than that per period, the rule wins. The same attitude applies to ties: prefer the simpler model unless the gain is clear.

#### Failure modes that do not announce themselves

Models fail quietly. Label delay: a problem record is known to be a problem only after weeks, so recent accuracy is unknown, and drift is your only early signal. Training and serving skew: a feature is computed one way for training and another way at prediction time, such as a median filled from the whole table in one place and from live rows in another. Feedback loops: if predictions change behaviour (flagged jobs get extra attention), the data you collect afterwards no longer looks like the data you trained on. Silent upstream change: a column is renamed, a unit changes from dollars to cents, a source goes stale.

For each, pick a monitor: PSI on inputs, score on recent matured outcomes, a freshness check on the source (see Part 4), and a range check on every input at prediction time.

#### Reviewing other people's models

A good review is a short list of questions, in order of risk. What is the decision this supports, and who bears the cost of a wrong answer? What is the baseline and how much does the model beat it? How was the split made? When does each feature become known? What changed between experiments? How wide is the uncertainty? What happens when inputs drift?

Worked example. A colleague reports F1 0.62 for problem prediction. You ask three questions: random or time split (random), baseline F1 (0.0, because the baseline never flags anything), and test positives (22). You rerun with a time split and get 0.31. You report findings as leads: "likely optimistic because of the split", not accusations, and you share the rerun so they can check it.

#### Honesty and ethics

You are usually the only person who knows how fragile the claim is, so you are responsible for saying it. State the baseline, the uncertainty and the period tested. Say what the model must not be used for. Be careful with proxies: even without a protected attribute, features such as postcode or a unit ID can stand in for one and produce unfair outcomes. Look at errors by group.

Do not tune until the number is good and then call it a finding, and do not describe an importance chart as proof of cause.

#### Operating at team scale

Every model has a named owner, a purpose in one sentence, a model card with its score against the baseline, a monitoring plan and a retirement rule. Changes go through review. Production promotion needs a second person to rerun the comparison on a period the first person did not use. Retraining is scheduled or triggered, never ad hoc and never forgotten.

Know when not to use ML: when a rule works, when there is no outcome data, or when nobody will act on the prediction. Real teams add authentication, audit and approval workflows; say so when you describe a personal project.

### Practice on your own

Take the model from Level 3 and treat it as a production candidate.

1. Set costs on paper (miss 200, false alarm 20). Compute the cost at three thresholds, pick one, and justify it in two sentences.
2. Write a one-page model card: purpose, data, baseline, score with range, known limits, and what it must not be used for.
3. Write a monitoring and retirement plan: which input and outcome checks, thresholds, the owner, and when the model is retired.
4. Swap roles: review your own earlier work (or a peer's) with the question list from this level, and write findings as leads with evidence.

### Exercises

1. A model saves 500 dollars a month and costs 800 a month to maintain. Decision?
2. List one monitor for each of: label delay, skew, feedback loop, upstream change.
3. Review checklist: write the five questions you would ask first.
4. A model reports an F1 of 0.80 with 10 positives in test. What do you say?
5. Compute the cost of a rule with FN 25, FP 60 at 150 per miss and 10 per alarm.
6. Name a proxy variable and the harm it could cause in an operations setting.
7. Write a retirement rule for a model.
8. Draft a three-sentence model card for the problem-record model.

### Answers and hints

1. Do not deploy: it loses 300 dollars a month. Revisit if either number changes.
2. Label delay: PSI plus a score on matured outcomes. Skew: compare training and serving feature summaries. Feedback loop: keep a small unflagged holdout. Upstream change: freshness and range checks.
4. Ten positives is too few: the range is very wide. Ask for more data or report a range.
5. 25 × 150 + 60 × 10 = 4,350 dollars.
6. For example, a zone code that stands in for a neighbourhood, leading to different false-alarm rates for different groups.
7. For example: retire when a replacement beats it on two periods, or when its owner leaves unreplaced.

Say aloud:

1. When does a simpler model win?
2. What is label delay and what do you do about it?
3. What is training and serving skew?
4. What are your first three review questions?
5. Why are proxy features a risk?

Common mistakes:

- Optimising the score while ignoring upkeep.
- Having no one assigned to the alert.
- Promoting a model reviewed only by its author.
- Accepting a feature because it helps the score without asking where it comes from.

## Capstone projects

### Capstone 1: Beat the baseline, honestly (small)

Brief: using any tool and a small dataset (your synthetic data from Level 1 will do), predict a numeric target and write a one-page note.

Deliverables: a table of three models against the baseline on a time split; one paragraph on which features you allowed and why; one leakage check you ran.

Rubric (score 0 to 2 each): baseline shown beside every score; time split used; each feature justified by "known beforehand"; a tie called a tie; limits stated.

### Capstone 2: A cost-aware problem flagger (medium)

Brief: predict "not completed" records with a threshold chosen from stated costs.

Deliverables: validation-based threshold choice; a cost table for at least three thresholds; confidence range on recall; a recommendation for an operations manager in plain words.

Rubric (0 to 2 each): threshold tuned off the test set; costs explicit; accuracy not used as the headline; uncertainty reported; recommendation readable by a non-specialist.

### Capstone 3: A tracked, registered, monitored model (large)

Brief: take one model from experiment to a monitored, versioned entry, and extend your training code with a new model type.

Deliverables: a tracked comparison of five models (MLflow or a run log) with a one-paragraph recommendation; a registered version in production with its card; three drift checks over time with a written reading; a new model type added to your training code, with a test; a monitoring and retirement plan.

Rubric (0 to 2 each): runs reproducible from notes; recommendation honest about ties; drift reading says "inputs" and not "accuracy"; code change tested and does not break existing models; plan names an owner, alert and retirement rule; a reviewer could rerun it.

|---|---|---|
| scikit-learn | Pipelines, classic models, metrics | Deep learning, very large data, or specialised models |

## Glossary

- **Baseline**: a trivial prediction (the average, or the majority class) that any model must beat.
- **Feature**: an input column the model may use.
- **Target**: the value being predicted.
- **Regression**: predicting a number.
- **Classification**: predicting a category, such as problem or no problem.
- **Time split**: training on earlier dates and testing on later ones.
- **Train, validation, test**: fit on train, choose settings on validation, report once on test.
- **Leakage**: information in the features that will not be available at prediction time.
- **Pipeline**: preprocessing and model bundled so both are fitted on training data only.
- **One-hot encoding**: turning a category into 0/1 columns.
- **Standardisation**: rescaling to mean 0 and spread 1 using training values.
- **MAE**: mean absolute error, in the target's units.
- **RMSE**: root mean squared error; punishes large misses.
- **R²**: share of variation explained relative to predicting the average; can be negative.
- **Confusion matrix**: counts of true and false positives and negatives.
- **Precision**: of those flagged, the share that were real.
- **Recall**: of the real cases, the share flagged.
- **F1**: the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
- **Threshold**: the probability above which a case is flagged.
- **Class weight**: extra importance given to the rare class during training.
- **Cross-validation**: repeated testing on different slices; time-ordered for time data.
- **Permutation importance**: score drop when one feature is shuffled.
- **Overfitting**: fitting noise, shown by a large gap between train and test scores.
- **Experiment tracking**: recording parameters, metrics and artifacts of each run.
- **Model registry**: named, versioned models with stages.
- **PSI**: population stability index, a measure of input distribution change.
- **Drift**: change in the data the model sees compared with training.
- **Model card**: a short description of a model, its score, limits and intended use.

## Career notes

Roles this part leads to: machine learning engineer, applied scientist, data scientist (modelling focus), MLOps engineer, analytics engineer with a modelling remit. Titles vary a great deal between organisations, so read the duties, not the title.

How to show your skill: publish the three capstones with their notes, written for a non-specialist. Show a baseline in every result, a leakage check, a cost-based threshold and a drift reading. A short write-up of a model you decided not to build impresses more than another leaderboard score. Be ready to explain each number aloud.

What to learn next: stronger statistics (Part 2), data pipelines that feed models (Part 4), serving and application wrappers (Part 5), then gradient boosting libraries, explanation tools and, if your work needs it, deep learning. Vendor and cloud ML certifications exist; check the current exam objectives before choosing one, and treat them as a supplement to a portfolio.

## Match it in the app

This section maps the part to the project's dashboard, for readers who have it running. Nothing above depends on it. Use the Practice workspace, which holds a made-up year of operations data (3,650 records, 10 units, 365 days, roughly 7 percent problem records) that you can break freely.

**Level 2.** Choose "Predict a problem record" with logistic regression and tick Units processed, Duration and Category. **What the app does not cover.** One time split only, so no repeated backtests. No outcome monitoring: drift looks at inputs only (PSI), not whether predictions were right. No hyperparameter search, no feature engineering beyond nine fixed features, no deep learning beyond a small neural network, no authentication or approval workflow in the registry, and models are joblib files, so load only your own folder. Review, ethics, model cards for others and team practice are things you do by hand.
